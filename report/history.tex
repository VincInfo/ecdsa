The Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), as an elliptic curve analogue of the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA), has a rich history marked by significant milestones in cryptographic development. In 1985 the mathematicians Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller introduced the foundational concept of Elliptic Curve Cryptosystems (ECC). The security of ECC's is based on the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) which is much more difficult than the traditional discrete logarithm problem. Therefore these systems provide enhanced security with smaller key sizes.

In response to NIST’s (National Institute of Standards and Technology) request for public comments on their initial draft for the Digital Signature Standard (DSS) ECDSA was first proposed by Scott Vanstone in 1992. This proposal set the stage for ECDSA's journey through various standardization processes.

ECDSA was first recognized as an International Standards Organization (ISO) standard (ISO 14888-3) in 1998. In the following year, it was accepted as a standard by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) under ANSI X9.62. The recognition of the ECDSA continued to grow when it was adopted as a standard by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) under IEEE 1363-2000 and as a Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS 186-2).

Unlike algorithms that rely on the ordinary discrete logarithm or integer factorization problems, the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem has no known subexponential-time algorithms, resulting in a much higher strength-per-key-bit ratio. This makes ECDSA a highly secure choice for cryptographic applications.

The widespread use and standardization of ECDSA underline its importance and effectiveness in digital signature processes, especially in environments where computing power, storage space and bandwidth are limited. ECDSA's ability to provide strong security with efficient implementation has made it a preferred choice in various cryptographic standards and applications.
